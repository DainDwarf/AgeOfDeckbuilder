# Simulator

> The headless simulator: the tool that plays chronicles through the rules from the terminal and reports numbers. A tool page beside [`PHASER.md`](PHASER.md), not a design page: the game it plays is [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md)'s, and the rules it plays by are [`CHRONICLE.md`](CHRONICLE.md)'s.

## What it is

A consumer of the rules through their public API: `apply` in a loop keeping the last stage's chronicle and reading the flow for what happened; no hook, field or flag lands in the rules or the content for it. It is a CLI under `tools/`, `npm run sim`, and its tests are Vitest on the fixture catalogue. It runs only when asked and reports numbers, never a diagnosis: a row says what happened, and the reading of why is the user's. No measurement runs on CI and none is committed.

It is not a player made of search: no within-turn search, no lookahead over turns, no value model over the map, no oracle of any kind. Why: a turn offers more orderings than a search can keep, and a value model is blind to whatever it does not name.

## The persona and the gambit

A **persona** is a stance a player could hold, written as an ordered list of **gambits**. A gambit is one condition and one command: read top down on every decision, the first gambit whose condition holds answers the command, the driver sends it through `apply`, reads the chronicle it leaves, and starts again from the top; the persona's last gambit ends the turn. A command refused or changing nothing is dropped and the walk goes on down the list. A chronicle runs to its ending, or is **stopped**, the stop noted as an error with its cause: on the turn cap's turn, a flag defaulting to 60, since the schedule has no last turn; at 100 commands landed in one turn; on a `runtime-error` among the launch's stages or a command's; and on a decision where no gambit's command lands, the end of turn's included. Why: a cap is a net for a loop in the content, a persona or the driver, never a budget, and a stop is a thing to investigate.

A gambit reads the chronicle the way the screen does and never opens a closure: it asks the rules' own question functions, the ones the screen asks, or plays a candidate command on the chronicle and reads the one it leaves, the game being pure. A trial whose chronicle carries another generator than the one it started on drew from it, which the player could not see, and the gambit reads nothing of that trial. A gambit weighs candidates one ply deep and no further. A gambit carries no value model. What it prefers is its sentence, an order of readings, the first deciding and the next of equals; where it must add, it adds a yield or the stocks with every resource counted as one, as the neutral's script sums a yield; no weight stands between two resources or two readings, and no number is scored over the map. Why: a weight is tuned beside the content and competes with it, and a sentence is read in the trace and struck. A reserve a gambit keeps is derived, never a constant: the dearest a card in hand or an answer the region's schedule could deal asks of that resource on this chronicle.

No gambit names a card, a building, a unit kind, an event or a capstone: it reads kinds, aims, costs, blocks, yields and counts, and a unit by what it does, a worker or one that attacks. A persona plays with the player's knowledge, the charted tiles, what is in sight and what the fog shows this turn, and reads the whole map nowhere. Every choice a gambit makes is deterministic on the seed: ties fall in tile order, and a gambit that draws draws from a generator seeded from the chronicle's seed, never from the chronicle's own.

Personas are lists in code, and the simulator prints a persona as its sentences. A second stance is a second persona, never a knob that makes the first optimal: where a persona is plainly wrong in one spot the trace shows it, and whether that is the persona's defect or the content's lesson is the user's reading.

A persona is as competent as one ply makes it inside its stance and blind outside it by design: a gambit changes when a trace shows a misplay a player of that stance would never make, never to win more. The numbers a persona carries, how many of a thing it keeps, how far it goes, are the persona's own.

**The goal** is what a persona's goal gambits go for: the achievement of the technology `--goal` names while it is unreached, and the capstone otherwise, so a cell with no goal named goes for the capstone from its opening, and so does one naming the age's victory, whose achievement is the capstone passed. Both personas carry the same goal gambits, ahead of the work on the land and behind the deal, the settle and the hazard, each reading the goal's count on the chronicle its trial leaves: the claim, the play, the worker's walk to where a play raises it, the unit's walk and the attack. Each answers nothing while no command of its kind raises the count, so a count that rises only at income or at the end of the turn is out of their reach and the persona plays as it would. A run with a goal answers whether a player going for it reaches it, and on which turn.

## The cell, the row, the query

A **cell** is an age, a region, a campaign and the civilization launched on it, a persona and a seed count. The campaign is built through the rules as the forge builds one: the technologies that reach the age and unlock the region learned first, each with its needs, then the ones the cell names. The civilization is the campaign's first, or one handed in whole as the content authors one, its city section and its deck, played as written: the technologies learned decide what the chronicle reads and replace nothing in it. The seeds run from 1 to the count, or over the range named, and pair across cells: seed _n_ is the same map and timeline under every persona and deck, and under the events and the capstone switched off or on, so a comparison between cells is a per-seed diff; the camps and the neutral's city are drawn before what the deal places after them, so a cell with either switched off deals another map than one with it on. A cell may be played with its region's camps, its neutral, its events or its capstone switched off, the content handed the rules changed for that cell alone — its region dealing no camp, naming no band for the neutral's city, its schedule's first deal or its capstone's turn due past any turn a chronicle reaches — so a reading isolates the economy from what opposes it; the capstone is still drawn at the opening, so the goal gambits go for it as ever; with the capstone off no chronicle ends before the cap, and the stop on the cap's turn is such a cell's ending, not an error. A cell may also be played with its region's schedule cut to the events named, each at the weight the schedule gives it, or with its capstone named instead of drawn, the content changed for that cell alone as when something is switched off; the timeline's generator steps once per draw whatever it draws among, so a cut schedule deals on the turns the whole one deals on, and a capstone named lands on the turn the seed would have drawn, or never where the capstone is off, the goal gambits then going for one trial on every seed. A cell may fix the answer taken to an event: whenever that event is dealt, the cell takes the answer named ahead of the persona's walk, the trace naming the knob where it names a gambit, and a deal on which that answer is refused stops the chronicle as stuck, the stop being the reading. What a cell cuts to, names or fixes is the region's schedule's own, and nothing is cut or fixed on a schedule whose events are switched off.

The cell is written once, as the first line of the file, a comment holding it as the command that made it: the age, the region, the civilization, whole where it was handed in, the technologies learned, the persona, the goal where one is named, the gambits left out where any is, in the order named, what is switched off where anything is, the events the schedule is cut to, the capstone named and the answers fixed where any are, each as given, every how many turns a row is read where `--every` names it, the turn cap and the seeds as given, so a file found later reads its cell whole, and the trace opens on the same line. One CSV **row** per chronicle, flushed as it lands: the seed, the ending or the stop with its cause and the turn it came on, the influence the ending paid, the capstone drawn and the turn it lands on, blank where the capstone is off; the city as the chronicle ended, its stocks, its population, the tiles it holds, the neutral's population, how and on which turn the neutral's city fell where it has, and the units standing, by faction and kind; the units killed, by faction and kind, the cards played with their count and the cards left in the piles never played, every deal with the entry taken and its turn, every achievement's count and the turn it was reached, and the time the chronicle took. With `--every` _n_, a chronicle writes one row more as each _n_th turn opens, from turn _n_, the chronicle as the persona's first decision of that turn reads it, under the same columns with the same meaning, what had happened by then, and the ending, its cause, what it paid and the time blank; the opening the ending or the stop reads writes no such row, the ending's row reading it. So a query draws the curve of any column over the turns, each turn's over the chronicles still standing on it. A row holds one value per column for what every chronicle of the cell has, and one cell listing what varies by seed, a listing being names each with its number. The rows are **queried** in place, in SQL through DuckDB's command line, reading past the cell's line, which the machine carries and the project does not depend on: one file's distributions, a cut of its rows by a condition, a curve over the turns, and two files joined on the seed, and on the turn too where both carry the rows over the turns. One seed replays with a **trace** naming the gambit behind every command. A **sweep** is one cell per value of a knob of the cell's own, and an **ablation** one cell per gambit, or set of gambits, left out of the persona's walk, nothing else changed; a cell past a handful of seeds runs as parts, a seed range each in its own process, and a run's files are read as one by the query.

## What its numbers say

It separates decks that differ in kind and reports how a deck fares as distributions of endings, failure modes and goals reached. It does not rank decks that differ by a copy or two: at a hundred seeds a rate carries about ten points of binomial error, the map and the timeline vary with the seed, and a persona plays a deck its own way.
